I took the master's entrance exam for Nara Institute of Science and Technology (NAIST) in July 2025, passed, and decided to enroll in April 2026. I’m writing down my whole experience here so it can be a reference for people coming after me.

Japanese grad school exam info is pretty closed off, and whether you pass often comes down more to how well you can gather information and a bit of luck. My experience only represents this year’s exam, but I hope it helps future applicants a little.

## My background

First off, the reason I focused on NAIST is simple: I’m fairly confident in my language ability during interviews, but I’m not good at grinding through problem sets. And my background is weaker than a lot of the heavy hitters out there. NAIST ended up being the best choice after weighing everything.

- Undergrad: a regular domestic non-elite full-time transfer bachelor’s degree in Software Engineering, GPA 2.95/4.0
- Japanese: JLPT N2 121 (basically winging it), after coming to Japan my listening and speaking got pretty strong, so I can handle everyday conversation in Japanese without trouble; before coming to Japan I passed N2 after only doing two practice tests
- English: TOEIC 810 (basically winging it), TOEFL 60 (winging it two years ago), I can explain things in my major, but everyday conversation is a mess; I can understand, but I can’t really speak

As you can see, the biggest disadvantage for me is my school background. And since I transferred from a junior college to a university, I never systematically studied a lot of the core subjects, especially higher math. During my five years in junior college + university, I didn’t have a single math class. The tiny bit of calculus I know came from cramming it during the transfer-exam period.

Also, as you can tell, I basically took every exam either totally unprepared or close to it. I really hate repetitive practice problems, so I’d study seriously and then just walk into the exam room and wing it based on my own ability. That means exams where test-taking tricks matter a lot basically left me clueless. On the flip side, once I do learn something, I usually understand it pretty solidly and can use it pretty flexibly — which doesn’t help much in exams, of course.

As for language tests, I passed them entirely on my own ability. Before TOEIC, I looked at the TOEIC “gold book” only to get familiar with the format and basic test-taking tricks — I didn’t even finish the listening section, and after I got 810 I just handed the book to a friend. Japanese was something I had already cleared before coming to Japan thanks to systematic study, daily listening/reading accumulation, and my 10-year veteran otaku battle record. So honestly, I don’t really have any tips to give there.

The only thing I’ll say is that TOEIC really has a ton of questions. I was rushing like crazy and finished with 5 minutes left, and that’s with my reading speed being pretty decent. When they collected the answer sheets, I peeked at the girl in front of me and saw that she’d filled in at least the entire last column of the answer sheet with the same answer. The TOEIC itself isn’t that hard — in my opinion, it’s at most around the difficulty of China’s CET-4. (By the way, my CET-4 and CET-6 were both completely unprepared too: I got 529 on CET-4, and 487 on CET-6 without even writing the essay.) But the hard part of TOEIC is speed, especially in reading. Long passages require really strong information-grabbing ability, or else you really can’t finish. When I do domestic CET practice tests, I usually still have 30–45 minutes left, just for reference. For TOEIC, I didn’t have time to read the later long passages at all — I just scanned a few lines, looked at the question stems, and hunted for info in the text. I didn’t even fully figure out the paragraph structure before hurriedly picking answers. Some people may always do English tests like this, but at least that’s not my style...

## Before applying

I came to Japan in October 2024 through language school and chose not to attend a cram school, relying entirely on self-study. At first I didn’t plan to aim for the imperial universities, but I did apply to Tokyo Tech. Later, after realizing I hadn’t done any practice problems and that there were a lot of hard questions, I just gave up and skipped the exam. As a backup, I interviewed for the international student entrance exam at Tokyo Denki University. The professor and I got along really well, but since I passed NAIST, I decided not to continue with the application.

### About Tokyo Denki University

There doesn’t seem to be much info about it online. I guess people only write experience reports after getting into better universities, so I’ll just write down what I went through here.

Tokyo Denki University is a private university, commonly grouped with the so-called “Four Engineering Universities,” and it’s often mentioned together with Shibaura Institute of Technology in the Tokyo metropolitan area. As of January 2025, when I’m writing this, the university is on the list published by immigration for the highly skilled professional points system.

If you take the general entrance exam there, you need to take written tests and test on some subjects. But internal students have a separate internal-selection track, so you don’t have to worry about competing with them for general-admission slots. As for international students, Tokyo Denki also has a dedicated international-student selection. The biggest difference from the general track is that there’s no written exam, only interviews — but after the formal application, it’s basically three interviews in total:

- International Center interview: this basically doesn’t assess technical ability; it checks language ability. At this stage you need to submit materials like your university transcript, diploma, degree certificate, research proposal, JLPT score, etc. Note that JLPT N2 is required
- Supervising professor interview: after passing the above, you interview with the professor you want to work with. At this stage you need to give about a 15-minute presentation on your proposed research, and the professor may ask questions
- Application: only after you get an informal acceptance from the professor through those two interviews can you formally apply and take the exam. The formal exam is also an interview

Before the professor interview and getting the informal acceptance, you’re not allowed to contact the professor directly. Everything has to go through the International Center. In my case, after I got the informal acceptance, the International Center gave me the professor’s contact info and told me I could contact the professor directly for academic questions.

It sounds easy, and in my experience it really was basically free. Of course, that might also be because my research proposal was pretty good and the topic almost perfectly matched the professor’s current research direction. During the professor interview, they barely asked me any technical questions. Instead, they talked about the lab’s current situation, the research content, the equipment, and said they hoped I’d take the exam. (Of course I later bailed, which I’m really sorry about. I had already told them I had passed NAIST, and the professor even joked that they’d been to NAIST for an academic presentation before, and that it’s really out in the sticks.)

So if the exam is that easy, where’s the biggest challenge?

International-student admissions require a Japanese residence status — in other words, you can’t just come on a tourist visa and take the exam directly.

So if you’re already in Japan, can accept private-school tuition, have decent language ability, and are confident in your research proposal, Tokyo Denki is actually a very solid backup option.

That’s it.

### Why I chose NAIST

Actually, among Japan’s two major science-and-technology graduate institutes, I heard about JAIST first, and only later learned about NAIST. When I first came to Japan, someone I knew here recommended NAIST to me. After looking into it, I found the school fit my situation really well, so I decided to make NAIST my main target.

But at first I struggled a lot with choosing a lab and a research plan. Later I found out NAIST also has an internship program, where you can do short-term research activities in a lab. Because of the very well-known reason that language schools keep an eye on attendance, I chose to spend two weeks at NAIST and took one week off from language school. The teachers at the language school supported me going, but that week couldn’t be counted as attendance.

I contacted a lab I was interested in directly and said I was interested in the internship. After we settled on the dates, I went through the application process with the school. If the school dorm has vacancies, you can actually stay there, but unfortunately there weren’t any empty rooms at the time. On the plus side, the school gave me accommodation and transportation subsidies. I ended up finding a hotel up on a hill nearby, and later found out the owner was a Malaysian Chinese guy, but that’s another story.

#### About the internship

For the internship, first you have to complete the fixed tasks for each internship program. Through that, you learn the lab’s basic research direction and pick up some foundational technical knowledge. After that, if you still have time left, you can do free research. During free research, you can use the school’s resources. On the last day, you need to give a presentation inside the lab, and the professor and other students will come listen.

I finished the fixed tasks pretty quickly during the first week. In the middle, I also sat in on a talk about AI programming, which made me a bit anxious, so based on paper reading I quickly settled on a research direction. In the second week, I determined my direction and immediately plunged into the world of model training, using the school’s GPU cluster to achieve my first-ever “model-cooking” achievement in life. I then gave a presentation on that content.

It was related to LLM fine-tuning, so I did get a model trained, more or less. But the data hadn’t been cleaned, and the resulting model was kind of ruined. Still, you can tell I definitely trained something.

In the end, I chose to present in Japanese and realized my grasp of technical terminology in information science was still pretty weak. And somehow I ended up speaking English even more than Japanese in the lab. Some of the senior international students weren’t Chinese and couldn’t speak Japanese, and they took really good care of me during the internship. Their academic and experimental skills were very strong, so my Japanese speaking ability actually got a bit worse over those few days. The professor also recommended that I apply in English, because if I can’t explain something well in Japanese during the interview, then in the actual exam it’ll be hard to tell whether it’s a language issue or whether I genuinely don’t understand it, and that would put me at a big disadvantage.

In short, even though it was only two weeks, I got a huge amount out of it. After I got back, I started writing my research proposal in English and had the senior lab members check it. Because of space limits, there were some things I couldn’t write about in detail, so I had to give them up.

If you’re already in Japan and interested in NAIST, and you’re thinking about applying, I strongly recommend taking part in the internship. It gives you time to talk with the professor, helps you decide your research direction, lets you meet senior lab members, gives you a feel for campus life, and there’s even a school subsidy. It’s basically a win all around with no downside.

### Pre-application screening

Note that foreigners who don’t have permanent residency need to go through a pre-application process before applying to NAIST. You need to complete it at least a month in advance. During this process, there may be a case where you need a preliminary interview with the professor to get the informal acceptance. Since I had already done the internship, I got the informal acceptance directly. Later, I met a Chinese classmate from the same lab who is also planning to enroll in April 2026, and she told me she also got the informal acceptance directly without an interview. So maybe my target lab just didn’t even look and sent it through...

Anyway, this pre-screening has to be done early, and the deadline is two months before the application, so make sure you don’t miss the timing.

## Exam subjects

Besides the interview, NAIST only tests math: calculus and linear algebra. The recommended textbooks are already provided. But they don’t count for that many points, and there are plenty of people who pass even after not solving a single problem, so you don’t need to stress too much.

The specified reference books are the famous MIT linear algebra introduction and old man Serge Lang’s *A First Course in Calculus*. The latter doesn’t have a Chinese edition, only Japanese and English versions. Note that the exam scope NAIST gives is based on the Japanese editions. The Japanese edition of MIT Linear Algebra is only the 4th edition, and the calculus textbook is only the 3rd edition. If you’re using the English versions, make sure you compare them carefully, because the chapter structure really is different.

If you have time to work through those two books, then definitely do all the exercises. NAIST’s math exam is basically just the exercises at the back of those books. But when I took it, I was unlucky and got two proof-style questions, and I only managed to solve one after the teacher gave me a hint.

I also used the Masema books as a supplement, but looking back, they weren’t really suitable for NAIST prep. If NAIST is your main target, just mastering the specified textbooks is more than enough. Especially with the calculus book, even if your high school math isn’t great, it’s fine — the author starts all over again from trigonometric functions. And there’s no epsilon-delta reasoning, which NAIST also doesn’t test anyway, so it’s actually very easy to understand.

I really regret not paying more attention to those two books, but since I passed, I guess it’s fine.

## The exam

The exam was fully online, using Cisco WebEx.

As for the formal exam process, there wasn’t much difference from the experience reports of people before me. After entering the room at the designated time and waiting, a teacher came out first to explain the rules. I instinctively greeted them in Japanese, so in the end the explanation was given to me in Japanese too（笑）. Anyway, they explained whether you’re also applying elsewhere and whether this is your first-choice school. They also said that this was just for survey purposes and wouldn’t affect whether you pass.

Then came math. You need to prepare paper so you can show your work. During the problem-solving, you can talk with the teachers, and they may even give hints, but when I got two proof questions, my brain completely froze. I got stuck for ages and didn’t say a word. In the end, with the teacher’s hint, I barely managed to solve the linear algebra one, and for calculus I only explained which theorem should be used before time ran out.

But like I said above, math isn’t heavily weighted. So no matter how well or badly you do on the math, the real key is the research-plan interview afterward. You absolutely have to switch gears fast and get ready for the interview.

During the interview, the professor for your first-choice lab sat in the middle, with two others on the sides. There was no presentation; they just started asking questions. Before it began, the professor casually chatted with me in Japanese, and then the formal interview started in English. Since I’d written the entire research proposal myself and was very familiar with this cross-disciplinary area, none of the questions were hard for me, and I answered everything smoothly. After the first-choice professor finished, they let the other professors ask questions. At the end, I exchanged a bit of small talk in Japanese again and then left the meeting room.

## Results

The results were posted on the official website at 10:00 a.m. sharp on July 25, and I happily found my exam number there. The score and acceptance letter could be downloaded from the application website a few days later.

My final score was 159/200, and this year’s cutoff was 131, so I was comfortably above it. It feels like I scored really high on the interview section.

## Some rumors about NAIST

This year, a lot of agents said NAIST had been kicked out of the list of schools that get extra points in the highly skilled professional visa system. As someone taking this year’s exam, I obviously had no motivation to correct that rumor — fewer applicants is no bad thing for me. But clearly I’ve already passed, so I figure I should clear it up and set the record straight.

About the points-eligible schools, the [Immigration Services Agency website](https://www.moj.go.jp/isa/applications/status/designatedactivities02_00004.html#midashi06) clearly explains it. You can see that the “list” many agents give is actually just the section called “universities eligible for points based on world university rankings.” NAIST is indeed not on that list right now. So where is NAIST? If you look at the second list, namely “universities receiving subsidies under the Super Global University Creation Support Project (Top Type and Global Traction Type),” NAIST is clearly there.

But as I keep saying, if you care a lot about international rankings, there just aren’t many Japanese schools for you to choose from. And the entrance exams at these top universities are also top-tier hard, so honestly I wouldn’t recommend studying in Japan for people who are that ranking-focused.
